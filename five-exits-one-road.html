Distance from the bumper = how much has to be true before you’re actually on that road — not how good it is. Dashed shoulder = a lane that runs with every road, not a road of its own. The hood = what you already carry. Digital systems is the vehicle; it drives on all five.

The road itself

unchanged by every exit

You asked for a map of where you’re going. Before the exits: this is the part of the map that no exit changes. If these six are true, any of the five roads will hold weight. If they aren’t, none of them will — and the bigger the platform, the faster it shows.

  • 01
    The hour nobody seesPrayer and the text when there is no talk to prepare and no one to tell. This is the only item on the list that cannot be faked for longer than about two years.
  • 02
    People who knew you beforeTwo or three who remember you without a title and have standing permission to ask you anything. Name them. If the list is empty, that is the first thing to fix, before any exit.
  • 03
    Somebody discipled to the third generationNot “I discipled him.” He discipled someone, and you weren’t in the room. This is the single measurement every one of the five roads shares.
  • 04
    The text, not your take on the textYou will drift toward whatever the room rewards. The discipline is preaching what’s actually there on a week when it isn’t what anyone came for.
  • 05
    Your household ahead of your ministryEvery road here has a documented way of eating this, and each one eats it differently. Decide the non-negotiables now, while they cost you nothing.
  • 06
    The thing you’d still do with no audienceWhatever remains when you subtract the platform, the salary and the invitations — that’s the gifting. Everything below is just a delivery vehicle for it.

The vehicle you’re already in

not a sixth exit

You added something after the map was drawn: using your digital experience to build out systems that actually work. And then asked whether that’s still building.

It is — but it builds capacity, not people. Scaffolding is building. A system can hold a hundred people; it cannot disciple one. So it counts, and it never counts instead. The measurement on this map doesn’t move: somebody you taught is teaching, in a room you weren’t in. Nothing you ship changes that number.

Which is why it isn’t drawn as a sixth road. Look at the picture again: the view is from the driver’s seat. The hood at the bottom of the frame is this. It’s the vehicle — the thing carrying you down whichever road you pick, and the reason some of those roads are reachable at all.

And it is not Exit 1. Building an audience makes you the point. Building systems makes you deliberately invisible — the work is good precisely when nobody notices it. Different gift, different risk, different payoff. Don’t let the lane eat the build.

Effect on exit 3

This is the difference-maker

  • Most bivocational planters take a job that owns their evenings — the exact hours a house church needs
  • Remote systems work is one of very few trades that pays a full living, travels to any city, and leaves those hours alone
  • If you go bivocational, this is almost certainly the job. Exit 3 just got materially more likely
Effect on exit 5

It cuts the raise

  • Every dollar you earn is a dollar you don’t have to ask for
  • Makes you a planter who survives a funding gap instead of folding in it
  • Networks are chronically under-built here — it’s also how you get known inside one
Effect on exit 4

Valuable fast — and that’s the trap

  • Most established churches run duct-taped systems and will notice you immediately
  • Which means they will hand you all of it, and you will be the operations guy by year two
  • If you take this road, say up front what you will not own
Effect on exits 1 & 2

Real, but low leverage

  • Itinerant: your calendar, contracts and follow-up stop being a mess. Useful, not decisive
  • Digital ministry: adjacent, and the easiest place to fool yourself that shipping is ministry
The gifting it eats

Seeing the whole flow before it exists, and caring whether it still works when you’re not the one running it.

What it costs

It is the most legitimate-sounding way in the world to avoid people. Building a discipleship tracker is not discipling anybody, and it feels almost identical from the inside.

Green light

Someone else’s ministry runs better because of something you built — and it keeps running after you stop touching it.

Red flag

A week where you shipped something and didn’t sit across from a single person.

Year one · 2026–27

Build one system end to end, for one ministry that isn’t yours, for free — and hand it off completely. Watch which part you actually enjoyed: the build, or the handoff. That answer tells you a great deal about all five roads.

Year two · 2027–28

Decide what this is: a trade (contract work, a real hourly number, a client or two) or a gift (free, occasional, capped). Both are fine. Running it as a gift while needing it to be a trade is what makes people bitter.

Year three · 2028–29

Either it is your bivocational income floor — a known monthly number that makes Exit 3 or Exit 5 survivable — or it’s something you do twice a year for people you love. Know which by now.

The five exits

tap a sign to isolate one
Exit 1

Digital ministry

Merge lane

What it isTeaching to an audience you do not pastor. It is on this map because you named it — but structurally it isn’t an exit. It’s a lane you can drive in on any of the other four roads, and the only one here with nobody to answer to.

Year one · 2026–27

One format, one audience, one question you keep answering. Ship three times a week for twelve months and do not look at the numbers weekly. Roughly 150 pieces before you are allowed an opinion about whether it works.

Year two · 2027–28

If it’s working: move people to an email list. Platforms are rented; a list is owned. Find the repeatable format and stop reinventing. First product, partnership or nothing — decide deliberately.

Year three · 2028–29

It either pays for part of your life or it is a free service you provide. Both are honorable. Pretending it’s one while running it like the other is what burns people out.

The gifting it eats

Compression — saying something true and costly in forty-five seconds without shaving the edges off it.

What it costs

The algorithm pays for heat. Over three years it will quietly reshape you into a person who says the thing that travels.

Green light

People who found you online turn up somewhere in person — a room, a table, a church.

Red flag

Your best hour this week went to the edit instead of to a person.

Why it’s drawn as a shoulder, not an exit: a house church planter, a pastor, an itinerant and a church planter can all drive in this lane. Treat it as a lane and it stops competing for a decision it was never going to win — and stops being the thing you do instead of the harder road.

Not to be confused with building systems: an audience makes you the point; a system makes you invisible on purpose. Your digital experience points at the second one far more than the first. See The vehicle you’re already in.

Exit 2

Revival itinerant speaker

High ceiling, no floor

What it isTraveling preaching — camps, conferences, campus, revival meetings. You carry a message into rooms you did not build and leave before the consequences arrive.

Year one · 2026–27

Say yes to everything small: youth groups, chapels, camps, jails, nursing homes, the 6am men’s breakfast. Target 25 times preached. Keep a log with one honest line each time — how you felt an hour after, not on the platform.

Year two · 2027–28

Forty to sixty dates. More importantly: a home church that sends you and elders who can ground you. What kills itinerants isn’t the travel, it’s that no structure exists that is able to say no to them.

Year three · 2028–29

Repeat invitations fill the calendar without you asking. Weeks-at-home per month fixed in advance and defended. A message you can preach on an empty tank without borrowing intensity.

The gifting it eats

Prophetic edge and sheer stamina — plus the rarer thing: the same man off the platform as on it.

What it costs

No congregation. Nobody sees your worst day. Statistically the highest-attrition road of the five, and the reason is structural, not moral.

Green light

The same places invite you back without you angling for it, and they invite you back after the hard sermon.

Red flag

You feel most yourself on a platform and least yourself at your own kitchen table.

Honest note: almost nobody starts here. This road is nearly always grown out of one of the other three, somewhere around year five to ten, when other people start asking for what you already carry. Trying to start here in year one usually means building an audience first — which is Exit 1 wearing a different sign.

Exit 3

Bivocational house church planter

Drew’s first answer

What it isA job pays your bills. The church multiplies in living rooms. You plant by making disciples who make disciples, not by launching a service — so the unit of growth is a room of twelve, and growth means a second room, not a bigger one.

Year one · 2026–27

Two moves at once. Pick the trade that travels and actually pays — and given what you already carry, that is almost certainly systems and digital work rather than a licensed trade or a teaching cert, because it’s the one that doesn’t own your evenings. Start it. And open your living room weekly for eight to twelve people, with a co-leader from week one, not week fifty.

Year two · 2027–28

Hand that room to the co-leader and start a second. The first multiplication is the entire test of this road; everything before it is just hosting. Get under a network with an actual coach (V3, Underground, Kairos, Salt — ask Drew which one he’d put you in).

Year three · 2028–29

Three or four rooms, and two leaders who came out of those rooms rather than being recruited into them. Your week is now shepherding leaders, not running meetings.

The gifting it eats

Apostolic initiative held at low visibility for a long time — starting things nobody will applaud, and giving them away early.

What it costs

Likely no salary from the church, ever. A split calendar. And nothing outside your city can see what you built, which is harder than it sounds at 29.

Green light

Someone you discipled disciples someone else, in a room you weren’t in and didn’t schedule.

Red flag

You picked it because it needs nobody’s permission, has no assessment, and nobody can fire you from it.

Why Drew may have said this first: it is the only road on the map you can begin testing this month, at nearly zero cost, without anyone’s approval — and it is the road that gives the most evidence about the other four. Ten weeks of a living room tells you whether you are a starter, a shepherd, or a speaker.

Exit 4

Pastor, established church

Inherited, not founded

What it isStaff or lead role in a church that already exists, full of people who were there before you and will be there after. You steward something you did not start — which is a different gift from starting one, not a lesser one.

Year one · 2026–27

Get a residency or internship at a church you’d be content to stay at for a decade. Preach anywhere they let you and keep the log. Learn the unglamorous half: hospital visits, budget meetings, the phone call at 11pm.

Year two · 2027–28

Start the ordination track in your tradition — exams, oral councils and paperwork all move slowly, so begin before you need it. Take an associate, college or youth seat and carry real weight in it.

Year three · 2028–29

Full-time staff. Forty to sixty sermons behind you. You have buried someone and married someone, and you know which one changed you more.

The gifting it eats

Longevity and week-in teaching — and the underrated capacity to be bored well without manufacturing drama to feel alive.

What it costs

You inherit someone else’s building, budget and history. Change is slow and paid for entirely in trust you have not earned yet.

Green light

Year three with the same people energizes you more than year one did. That reaction is rare and it is the whole qualification.

Red flag

You’re drawn to it mainly because it’s the option with a salary and a title attached.

Worth noticing: this is the one road on the map you cannot simply choose. It requires a call from a congregation. That makes it the slowest to test and the easiest to drift into by default while waiting for the others to clarify.

Exit 5

Traditional church planter

Drew’s second answer

What it isA launch team, a public gathering, outside funding, a named church in a named place. You are a founder, and for the first two years the job is far more organizational than pastoral.

Year one · 2026–27

Get assessed. Every network runs a gate — Acts 29, NAMB, ARC, Stadia, EFCA, your own denomination — and the queues are long, so start now. Meanwhile serve under a planter, not just a pastor. The two jobs are not the same job.

Year two · 2027–28

Residency, typically 12–24 months, and this is the year that decides it. Raise support (a three-year plant budget commonly lands somewhere around $150k–$350k). Build a core team of 15–25. Pick the city and actually move there.

Year three · 2028–29

Pre-launch or launch. You are now running a small organization: budget, bylaws, insurance, kids ministry, a staff hire, a lease. Preaching is maybe a fifth of the week.

The gifting it eats

Casting vision to strangers, asking people for money without flinching, and executive follow-through when nobody is checking.

What it costs

Two years as a fundraiser and administrator. The highest financial risk of the five. Attrition here is driven by marriage and money far more than by theology.

Green light

People follow you somewhere costly before there is anything built to join — when all you have is the idea.

Red flag

You want the launch Sunday more than you want year five.

The overlap with Exit 3: these two roads share almost everything for the first eighteen months — make disciples, gather a core, get assessed, find a coach. They diverge on one question only: who pays for it. Which means you do not have to answer this one yet, and you shouldn’t force it.

What the map already shows

before you run a single test

  1. Professor came off the board without a fight. When the five were laid out, that one didn’t survive the first pass. That’s real information — a PhD and a decade of adjuncting deserve a harder yes than you were willing to give it. Down to four.

  2. Both of Drew’s answers were planting. He didn’t say pastor. He didn’t say speaker. He didn’t say platform. Whatever he sees in you, he sees a starter — and he’s watched you longer than a page like this can.

  3. So the real fork is two questions, not five. First: do you start something, or steward something someone else started? Then, only if you start it: who pays for it — a job, or donors? Everything else on this map is downstream of those two answers.

  4. Digital ministry isn’t competing with the other four. It’s a lane on all of them. The danger isn’t that you choose it — it’s that it quietly becomes the thing you do instead of the harder road, because it gives back a signal every single day and the harder roads give nothing back for a year.

  5. The sixth thing you named isn’t a road — it’s the engine. Building systems that work is the only item you’ve mentioned that makes the two roads Drew named more reachable rather than competing with them. It answers the question Exits 3 and 5 actually diverge on: who pays for it. That makes it the most strategically useful thing on this page, and the least likely to be what you’re called to.

  6. Itinerant is a destination, not a departure. Nearly everyone who does it well arrived there from one of the other three. If it’s truly yours, it will come and find you around year five, and it will come through people who watched you pastor somebody.

  7. The three years are shorter than they look. Assessment queues, ordination exams and residencies all run 12–24 months and none of them start when you decide — they start when you apply. Whatever you pick, the paperwork for it should be moving by mid-2027.

The vanishing point isn’t a career. All five roads end in the same place, and not one of them is the point — which is the only reason it’s safe to pick one.
